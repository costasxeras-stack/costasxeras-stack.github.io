KostasTravel

A hotel room reaches a booking site through several suppliers, each with its own name for it. A mapping layer decides which master room every supplier rate belongs to, and the guest sees that room's photos, beds and amenities. I wanted to find where this goes wrong, and how a guest would notice.

Approach

  1. Built the guest view first: a complete booking site, from search to payment and cancellation.
  2. Built an explorer for the machinery. For each hotel it puts the content catalogue (every master room) next to the rates (every supplier rate and the room it was mapped to).
  3. Scanned a real market: Kraków, 12 to 14 December 2026, 2 adults. I looked in depth at the 40 hotels with the most offers.
  4. Checked every candidate by hand before calling it a finding: room names, prices, bed lists and the photo files.

Findings

Confirmed in sandbox data

The same room stored twice, with prices on the empty copy

Hotel A holds two master rooms called "Classic Plus Queen": same bed, same 19 sqm, same six photos. All 4 live rates link to the record with 0 amenities and a single line of description. The record with 41 amenities and a full description never gets a price, so no guest sees it. The data exists. The merge is missing.

Confirmed in sandbox data

Two room tiers mapped the wrong way round

At Hotel B, the supplier's "Deluxe" rate (about €423) is mapped to the Grand Deluxe room, and the "Grand Deluxe" rate (about €475) to the Deluxe room. A guest paying for one is shown the other. The two rooms share three photos, so a reviewer looking at pictures would not catch it.

Confirmed in sandbox data

Different price tiers, the same pictures

At Hotel C, the Standard twin (16 m²) and the Deluxe twin (25 m²) share 8 of 9 photos. The Deluxe costs about €120 more for two nights, and the content gives a guest no way to see why.

Hypothesis

Duplicates come from a second content source

The record IDs suggest the richer content was added as a new room instead of being merged into the existing one.

What I learned as a PM

  • Coverage can look perfect while quality fails. 100% of rates were mapped, and guests could still see the wrong room or an empty one.
  • Look at the product the way the guest does, then go one layer down.
  • My own keyword rules flagged false positives ("Classic" mapped to "Standard" is a synonym, not an error). Anything built on rules needs a human review step before it counts.

What I would do next

  1. Detect duplicate master rooms automatically, by bed type, size, shared photos and name similarity, with a human review band.
  2. Define survivorship rules that keep the best source field by field.
  3. Flag tier swaps with a price signal.
  4. Prioritise fixes by booking value, not by count.

Limits: sandbox data, one market, one date range, 40 hotels. The counts show each pattern exists, not how common it is. Free hosting can take a minute to wake up.
